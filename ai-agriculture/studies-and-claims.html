Understanding scalability assesment. Can an intervention reach more producers and sustain benefits at a feasible cost? The assessment considers sustained use, costs and funding, delivery, use in other settings, who benefits, and lasting benefits and wider effects.

What the labels mean. “Not reported” means the reviewed material does not provide that information. “Not yet reviewed” means the review is incomplete. Neither means that scaling is impossible. Reviews and background studies may have no specific intervention to assess.

Ethiopia video extension

Finding: In Ethiopia, adding local videos to public extension increased reported uptake of recommended cereal practices by about 6–7 percentage points over two seasons; sustained yield gains were not established.

Intervention: Agent-facilitated local videos added to public extension, with or without separate spouse sessions.

Counterfactual: Conventional government extension targeting household heads, using verbal instruction, discussion and farm visits to promote the same practices. Video delivery also standardized local examples and peer demonstrations; this was not an isolated test of video images or AI.

Study design: Three-arm cluster-randomized trial, with kebele assignment stratified within 30 purposively selected expansion woredas in four regions, during the 2017/18 and 2018/19 meher seasons. Intention-to-treat estimates include woreda fixed effects and kebele-clustered standard errors.

Sample: Initially selected: 350 kebeles and 2,450 households. First survey: 347 kebeles and 2,422 households. Retained panel: 342 kebeles and 2,345 households (77 lost between surveys). Adoption regressions use 2,345 observations in year 1 and 2,119 in year 2; the main text does not explain the additional second-year exclusions.

Key limitation: Video delivery continued in both seasons. Selected districts, mostly male-headed households and self-reported adoption limit transfer; lower costs at full saturation are projected.

Follow-up: Two meher seasons, 2017/18 and 2018/19, with household surveys in January–March 2018 and January–March 2019. Video delivery continued in both seasons; there is no post-withdrawal persistence test. Long-run effects and possible novelty effects remain unresolved.

What this study tests: Extension delivery is tested; agronomic knowledge is the pathway.

Access to advice: Household heads were 16.9 percentage points more likely to receive advice or training on any promoted practice in year 1 (SE 2.03; control 47.2%) and 8.14 points more likely in year 2 (SE 2.63; control 42.6%); both p < 0.01. Increased farm-visit effects were not statistically established in year 2.

Pages: Table 5, p. 9; mechanisms discussion, pp. 8–9.

Choices and adoption: Pooled treatment increased reported uptake of any promoted practice by 6.24 percentage points in year 1 (SE 1.87; control 63.6%; n = 2,345) and 7.39 points in year 2 (SE 2.49; control 66.4%; n = 2,119); both p < 0.01. Row planting and precise seeding effects persisted under continued delivery; the second-year urea-dressing effect was not statistically significant.

Pages: Table 4, p. 8; uptake definition, p. 6.

Choices and adoption: Adding spouse-targeted sessions did not significantly increase adoption beyond household-head-only video sessions (any-practice equality tests: p = 0.153 in year 1; p = 0.557 in year 2). This does not establish that inclusion has no value; spouses received the same content rather than videos tailored to women.

Pages: Table 4, p. 8; intervention footnote 8, p. 4; discussion, p. 9.

Production: No robust sustained pooled yield gains were established. Teff showed a marginally significant year-1 gain (p < 0.10), while pooled wheat and maize effects were not significant; all pooled crop yield effects were insignificant in year 2. Harvests were self-reported, with self-reported or GPS-measured areas; crop cuts were not conducted. These results do not establish profit, income or welfare gains.

Appendix H was not retrieved, so detailed cost coverage and scaling assumptions remain unverified.

Study details (6 experiments)

KALRO

Finding: KALRO’s agronomic SMS did not establish an increase in lime purchases in the following season.

Intervention: Twenty-one human-authored SMS covering lime, fertilizers, seeds, and field management; lime advice was conditional on soil acidity and recommended soil testing.

Counterfactual: No advisory SMS. Both groups received 50% lime and fertilizer discount coupons after the endline survey.

Study design: Farmer-level randomization to agronomic SMS or no SMS in Kakamega and Siaya; messages during the 2015 short rains, survey after that season, and coupon purchases before the 2016 long rains.

Sample: 832 farmers randomized; 773 in the final Table 2 analysis sample. The lime-purchase logit uses 674 observations; the linear-probability model uses 773.

Key limitation: The purchase outcome was measured after messaging ended and included a 50% discount coupon. An imprecise estimate does not establish no effect; yields, profits, and soil restoration were not demonstrated.

Follow-up: 2015 short-rains survey and 2016 long-rains coupon purchases. No second-season advisory texts were sent.

What this study tests: SMS recommendations are tested; soil inputs are the decision context and extension is the delivery channel.

Choices and adoption: Next-season lime purchases: odds ratio 0.87 (95% CI 0.53–1.42), without a statistically established increase. The separate linear-probability estimate is −1.1 percentage points (SE 2.2).
